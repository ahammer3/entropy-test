/// A dice experiment in five parts

“Just make it random.”

It sounds like the easiest instruction in programming. It is one of the hardest things a computer actually does — and when it’s done wrong, nothing looks wrong. That’s the whole trap. Below, five short experiments with a single six-sided die show you why.

Experiment 01 · The eye test

Two dice. One is honest.

One of these streams comes from your operating system’s cryptographic randomness. The other is a five-line function a developer wrote in a hurry — the kind that ships to production all the time. Watch them roll. Which stream is truly random?

Finding

You cannot verify randomness by looking at it. A predictable generator and a genuine one produce output that is, to the human eye, identical.

Experiment 02 · The hidden lattice

Plot it differently, and the ghost appears.

Take that same “hasty” generator. Instead of reading the dice one at a time, plot each roll against the next roll as a point on a square. Genuine randomness fills the square like static. The hasty generator… doesn’t. Its numbers were never free — they were quietly marching along a handful of invisible lines the whole time.

Finding

Flaws that are invisible in one dimension can be glaring in two. A generator can pass every casual check and still live on a lattice you just weren’t looking at.

Experiment 03 · The seed is the secret

If the starting point is guessable, everything is.

Every software generator is just a machine turning a crank from a starting number — the seed. Same seed, same dice, every single time. So the only thing protecting you is how hard the seed is to guess. Here a “device” picked a secret seed from a small pool and leaked its first ten rolls. Watch an attacker try every possible seed until the rolls line up — and then predict rolls the device hasn’t even made yet.

Finding

The math can be flawless and it won’t matter. If the seed comes from a small or predictable source — a clock, a counter, a weak fallback — the entire stream of “random” numbers is reconstructable. This is the exact failure class behind real-world key-generation disasters: not broken cryptography, broken entropy.

Experiment 04 · Death by a rounding error

Even perfect randomness can leak through a bad shortcut.

Now suppose your randomness is genuinely perfect — a flawless source that hands you a digit, 0 to 9. To turn it into a die you write the obvious thing: value % 6. Ship it. But 10 doesn’t divide evenly by 6: the values 6, 7, 8, 9 wrap back onto faces 1, 2, 3, 4 — so those four faces land twice as often as 5 and 6. A perfect source, a one-line habit, and a quietly loaded die.

Finding

Good randomness is a chain, and every link counts: the source, the seeding, and the way you shape raw bits into the values you need. A one-line convenience can quietly undo all of it.

Experiment 05 · You try

“I’ll just pick randomly in my head.”

The instinctive human fallback is the worst generator of all. Tap the dice as randomly as you possibly can — really try. A dead-simple predictor that only knows your last move will guess your next one. If you were truly random it would be right 1 in 6 times (16.7%). Watch it beat that.

People avoid repeats, over-alternate, and dodge patterns they think “look rigged.” Those very habits are the pattern. Intuition is not an entropy source.

/// So how do you actually do it?

You don’t invent randomness — you harvest it. Real systems collect genuine physical unpredictability (electrical noise, timing jitter, dedicated hardware) into an entropy pool, then use a cryptographically secure generator so that seeing past outputs tells you nothing about future ones. The rule of thumb for everyone else is short: never roll your own. Reach for the vetted primitive your platform already ships — crypto.getRandomValues, /dev/urandom, secrets — not Math.random, not a timestamp, not your own clever function.

Because that’s the thread running through all five experiments: broken randomness doesn’t announce itself. It passes the eye test, hides its lattice, reuses a guessable seed, and leaks through a rounding error — all while looking perfectly fine. “Just make it random” is a genuinely hard problem wearing an easy problem’s clothes.
